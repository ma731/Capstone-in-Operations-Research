The Price of Sophistication

Explained as clearly as possible. Every side, every decision, in plain words.

The whole thing in 20 seconds

You run data centers in several regions. Every evening you choose where tomorrow's flexible work runs (things like AI training that can wait a few hours or run somewhere else). You do this to keep a carbon promise: use cleaner electricity.

Electricity is cleaner at some hours and places than others, because of sun, wind, and demand. So the smart move is simple: send the work to the cleanest hour and the cleanest place.

Salespeople offer you three "smart" upgrades on top of that. This thesis prices each one. The answer: one pays, two do not.

Decision 1

Should you move work between regions?

YES · do it

In plain words: is it worth building the ability to shift work to whichever region is cleanest right now?

The case for it

At any given hour, somewhere is cleanest. If you can send your work there instead of leaving it where it is, you burn less carbon. Obvious win, if it works.

What we actually found

It works, and it is the big one. Moving work cuts your worst-day emissions by 4 to 10% (Western 4.0%, Eastern 9.9%, Diversified 9.0%). On one fleet that is roughly $1.7M a year (a gross figure, before the cost of moving the work). And it needs zero fancy statistics.

In one sentence: this is the lever. Build it.

Decision 2

Should you model how the regions move together?

NO · skip it

In plain words: the regions' cleanliness rises and falls together. Should you build a model of that linkage to schedule even smarter?

The case for it

The regions really are linked. The correlation runs up to 0.78 out of 1 on the US West, which is close to a perfect match. It feels obvious that capturing that linkage would help. The whole research field assumed it does, and sold you the model on that assumption.

What we actually found

It adds almost nothing: under 0.4%, which is below the noise, and worth maybe $37k a year that is not even reliably positive. Sometimes it even hurts. So: skip it.

The twist (this is the interesting part)

The linkage signal is not fake. When we artificially removed the big daily pattern, the very same model suddenly paid +1.46%. So the signal is real. It is just drowned out.

Why? Carbon has a huge, predictable daily wave (driven by the sun and demand). The cross-region linkage is a tiny ripple riding on top of that wave, about 2 to 4 times smaller. Your schedule follows the giant wave, and the little ripple is simply too small to change its mind.

Picture it: trying to change a song's volume with a whisper while a loudspeaker is blasting. The whisper is real. It just cannot compete.

In one sentence: the signal is real, but too small to bank. Skip the model.

Decision 3

Should you plan for a worse day than the forecast?

ONLY IF · conditional

In plain words: forecasts are wrong sometimes. Should you pay a small premium every day to be protected on the bad days? This is basically insurance.

The case for it

If tomorrow turns out much dirtier than predicted, a plan that trusted the forecast gets hurt. Insurance protects you on those bad days, for a small everyday cost. Sounds prudent.

What we actually found

The insurance only pays once carbon can spike to about 3 times normal (a tripling). And real grids do not get there. The worst spikes we measured across 17 zones over 5 years had a median of about 1.4 times. Even Winter Storm Uri, the worst grid emergency in the data, only reached 1.28 times. So on real data: do not buy it.

In one sentence: insurance only pays for disasters that do not happen on these grids.

The bottom line, in four lines

  1. Always Ride the daily wave. Schedule each region day-ahead. This is free.
  2. The lever Add the ability to move work between regions. 4 to 10% off your worst days.
  3. Skip The co-movement model. Under 0.4%, below the noise.
  4. Only if Buy robustness only if you expect emergencies past a tripling. Real grids are not there.

"A fancier model is not safer. It is a bill, and it only pays when the ripple rivals the wave."

Why you can trust the two "no"s

A "we found nothing" result is easy to doubt, so it was built to be checked:

  • The rule was locked first. The pass-or-fail line went into version control before we ever looked at the test year (2025).
  • We proved the test works. Remove the daily wave and the same test fires at +1.46%. So a "no" is a real no, not a broken instrument.
  • The test was powerful enough. It could have caught any real effect down to about 0.1%. It caught nothing above 0.4%. That is "we proved it isn't there", not "we didn't look hard enough".
  • Everything is receipted. 202 automated tests, and every number on every slide traces to a saved file.

The few words, decoded

Worst-day emissions (CVaR)
We score the average of your dirtiest 5% of days, not a normal day, because a carbon promise fails on the bad days.
Gross (as in "$1.7M gross")
The saving before subtracting the cost of moving the work (network, delays). It is a ceiling, not a net profit.
The lever
The one upgrade that pays: moving work between regions.
The wave and the ripple
The wave is the big daily swing in how clean power is. The ripple is the small cross-region linkage on top. The wave is 2 to 4 times bigger, so it wins.
The positive control (+1.46%)
A deliberate check that the test can detect a real effect. It can. That is what makes the "no" trustworthy.
